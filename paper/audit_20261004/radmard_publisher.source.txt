Croft 1 934 Accesses 15 Citations 2 Altmetric Explore all metrics Abstract When a robotic visual servoing/tracking system loses sight of the target, the servo fails due to loss of input. To resolve this problem a search method, namely a lost target search (LTS) which will generate efficient actions to bring the target back into the camera field of view (FoV) as soon as possible, is required. For high dimensional platforms, like a camera-mounted manipulator or an eye-in-hand system, such a search must address the difficult challenge of generating efficient actions in an online manner while avoiding kinematic constraints. In this work, we utilize the latest available information from the target just prior to leaving the FoV to initiate an optimal online search. We explain various features of our overall LTS algorithm and provide simulation comparisons with common methods existing in the literature. Finally, we implement and demonstrate the capabilities of our general algorithm on a laboratory scale 7 degree of freedom (DoF) eye-in-hand system tracking a fast moving target. Intervision Object vision Rehabilitation Robotics Targeted resequencing Robotics Robotic Engineering Active Visual Object Search in Dynamic Environments 1 Introduction Having a camera as the main, or the only, feedback sensor of a robotic platform has become widely appealing in areas like surveillance, industrial manipulation, rescue robots, soccer robots, planetary rovers, etc. (Kragic and Vincze 2009 ). These various applications for surveillance, manipulation, rescue and servoing share the common task of object tracking. Such a task involves object detection, trajectory estimation and hardware manipulation, all of which depend on some visual cues of the target. In the context of a single mounted camera platform servoing to, or tracking, an object, most research has focused on maintaining the target within the camera FoV (Nelson and Khosla 1995 ; Chesi et al. 2003 ; Murrieta-Cid et al. 2005 ; Panagou and Kumar 2014 ). However, due to factors such as limited camera FoV, system constraints, occlusions, and poor lighting conditions, maintaining visibility is not always possible. For example, during visual servoing in Kragi ( 2001 ), if the target leaves the FoV or is occluded, a search is launched only in the image space until the target is found or the time spent on searching passes a predefined time constraint. In other words, if the target does not reappear in a predictable and narrowly localized site, such algorithms will fail. The ability to robustly look for and find truly “lost” targets is a natural next step to improve robot autonomy in wide range of applications. In this paper we present a fast and efficient online planner—an adaptive planner that updates and replans as new information arrives—to search for a moving target when a high dimensional camera mounted robotic platform loses track of the moving object. Without loss of generality, we consider a camera-mounted manipulator—a 7-DoF eye-in-hand system—as our exemplar high dimensional robotic platform while the target moves in 3D as depicted in Fig. 1 . When the target state is not observable, probabilistic models capture uncertainties in both the target location and sensory observations. Then a Recursive Bayesian Estimation (RBE) is required to incorporate the new target information provided by new observations. A planner or decision maker is also required to plan optimal sensory observations. This process is the foundation of probabilistic search strategies originating from operational research work (Koopman 1979 ; Stone 1975 ). Figure is generated using Barrett Technology WAM CAD model A review of recent advancements in autonomous robots identified two closely related fields: search and pursuit-evasion (Chung et al. 2011 ). In search—also known as one-sided search—the target is unaware of, and moves independently of, the searcher agent state (Bertuccelli and How 2006 ; Chung and Burdick 2012 ); however, in pursuit-evasion—also known as adversarial search—an evader with full awareness of the searcher state creates the worst-case adversary (Gerkey et al. 2006 ; Hollinger et al. 2010 ). In this work we focus on a specific type of one-sided search problem called lost target search (LTS). In LTS a recent history of the target is available, and rapid recovery of the lost target through online and efficient actions is important. A related area of research, where a robotic platform with a single, limited-range sensor applies various searching methods, involves unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), where one or more UAVs search for one or more lost targets (at sea or in the countryside). One of the early implementations of UAVs’ search for a single target is presented in Bourgault et al. ( 2004 ) using a grid-based RBE. Further improvements were later provided by the same group in Lavis et al. ( 2008 ), Chung and Furukawa ( 2009 ). Considering the same 2D platform, other forms of RBE and optimal planners were applied in Ryan and Hedrick ( 2010 ), Hollinger and Sukhatme ( 2014 ), Lin et al. ( 2014 ) among others. Various search algorithms have also been implemented on wheeled mobile robot platforms equipped with mounted sensors (usually a camera) to search for an object in an indoor environment. A stereo camera is the only sensor mounted on a mobile robot platform in Shubina and Tsotsos ( 2010 ), Ma et al. ( 2011 ) to search for a stationary target in an indoor environment. The work in Aydemir et al. ( 2013 ) further improves the object search to large dynamic cluttered indoor environments by utilizing environment semantics and additional sensors for obstacle avoidance. There are a few applications of search methods on robotic manipulators. A sample-based motion planning was developed in Espinoza et al. ( 2011 ) for an eye-in-hand system with mobile base to search for a stationary target in a known 3D indoor environment. This approach is presented in simulation, and its offline planning process is stated to be time consuming as it covers the whole environment. In contrast, real world implementation of a novel, multistage optimization algorithm to search for partially observed or unobserved stationary targets in 3D environment using multi-DoF eye-in-hand systems is presented in Zhang et al. ( 2014 ). While this approach considers robot joint limits and singularities, it is slow even for stationary targets due to the adopted image-based deterministic cost functions. To speed up the process, a probabilistic search is suggested in Webb and Furukawa ( 2008 ) for robotic manipulators, but only simulation results of a manipulator searching for a moving target in 2D are presented. While an eye-in-hand manipulator platform often has six or more DoFs, the majority of the probabilistic search techniques in robotics focus on 2D search. The first implementation of a 2.5D multi-agent target search with height maps is claimed in Kolling et al. ( 2010 ). Recently, sample-based viewpoint planning techniques are adopted in Aydemir et al. ( 2013 ), Espinoza et al. ( 2011 ) for mobile robots/manipulators to search for a stationary target in 3D. In addition, Webb and Furukawa ( 2008 ) simulates searching for a moving target in 2D and mentions development of more general search algorithms for higher dimensions as future work. In a survey of recent search and pursuit-evasion advancements in the mobile robotics field (Chung et al. 2011 ), the extension of efficient algorithms for practical search into 3D and higher dimensions is mentioned as an open problem. Also, bounded speed, constrained mission endurance and refined sensing models are listed as ongoing practical challenges in the field, and these are some of the problems that we try to tackle in this work. From a complexity perspective, the active object search is shown to be non-deterministic polynomial-time hard (NP-hard) in general—even considering a single agent searching for a single target (Andreopoulos and Tsotsos 2009 )—while under various simplifying assumptions, it is proven to be NP-complete (Andreopoulos and Tsotsos 2009 ; Ye et al. 2001 ). This problem becomes increasingly intractable as the spatial dimensions of the sensor and target states as well as the temporal dimensions of planning process increase. High-DoF searching agents, target motion in higher dimensions, and planning over a time horizon (beyond one step) all add to the complexity of the problem. Therefore, an approximate solution is all one can guarantee, and we aim to find a sub-optimal algorithm that can search efficiently in an online fashion. This algorithm should avoid the curse of dimensionality encountered in RBE and optimize a high dimensional cost function to plan a trajectory with a reasonable planning horizon. In this work we develop an online planner for probabilistic search that is applicable to a higher dimensional search with constraints, where the agent and target state spaces are different. We introduce an observation model that forms the bridge between the agent space and the target space. We implement a Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) method (Doucet et al. 2001 ) to handle nonlinear systems with non-Gaussian noise models in RBE. During this process we compare different approximate solutions with some common cost functions and planners to drive the search on our eye-in-hand platform. Contributions of this work are as follows: (1) Introducing a novel observation model that considers the camera boundary and depth effects. (2). Developing a fast online search algorithm that is applicable to high dimensional search for a moving target in 3D. (3) Introducing a cost function that avoids the kinematics constraints of a multi-DoF robotic platform. These three contributions are implemented both in simulation and on a physical eye-in-hand platform, and comparisons of our online search algorithm with relevant, previously published, algorithms are provided. In the following section we provide some necessary background in the LTS field. In Sect. 3 we present our problem in detail. Then we present our methodology and elaborate our contributions in Sect. 4 followed by our results in Sect. 5 . These last three sections are presented in similar subsections to highlight different aspects of our contributions. Finally we conclude and discuss future research avenues in Sect. 6 . 2 Related work A probabilistic search is structured around target belief state evolution and sensor optimal trajectory planning. A target belief state—posterior distribution of the target state also known as filtering density—evolves by RBE while relying on two main sources of information. One is the target’s a priori information, obtained from tracking before losing sight of the target in our case, and the other source is new observations. In addition, an RBE contains two stages of prediction and update, where it propagates the target state by a state transition model and updates the belief state using an observation model through Bayes’ rule. In this process the target’s a priori information is presented by a probability density function (PDF) and both the state transition model and observation model contain noise terms. As both the target and the sensor have motion models, we will continue to use the term sensor motion model but will refer to the target motion model as the state transition model throughout the paper. Depending on the noise terms and the models, closed-form and approximate solutions exist for RBE (Simon 2006 ). For example linear models with Gaussian noise terms can employ Kalman Filter (KF) with closed-form solution for the target’s belief state. If the models are nonlinear or the noise terms are non-Gaussian, then approximate solutions exist (Arulampalam et al. 2002 ). For example, Extended Kalman Filters (EKF) and Unscented Kalman Filters (UKF) are used when the system is non-linear but the noise terms are still Gaussian. In the presence of non-Gaussian noise in nonlinear systems, approximate grid-based methods (like Hidden Markov models) are used when the continuous state space can be decomposed into grids (cells), otherwise SMC is applied. Even though EKFs are used for tracking a moving target and localizing a stationary target as in Zhou et al. ( 2008 ), Vander Hook et al. ( 2014 ), most of the LTS literature relies on the last two methods to solve RBE. Approximate grid-based methods are used extensively in 2D and simplified 3D scenarios (Bertuccelli and How 2006 ; Chung and Burdick 2012 ; Bourgault et al. 2004 ; Lavis et al. 2008 ; Lin et al. 2014 ; Shubina and Tsotsos 2010 ; Ma et al. 2011 ; Aydemir et al. 2013 ; Webb and Furukawa 2008 ; Tisdale et al. 2009 ; Hollinger et al. 2009 ), but suffer from the curse of dimensionality. In higher dimension applications, particle filters (i.e., SMC) are more promising since their inherent randomization of the samples breaks this curse. Particle filters are also widely used in Chung and Furukawa ( 2009 ), Ryan and Hedrick ( 2010 ), Ramirez et al. ( 2014 ), and Geyer ( 2008 ), and in Radmard and Croft ( 2011 ) we demonstrate their superior performance compared to approximate grid based methods. For both of these reasons we select a particle filter to approximate the RBE in this work. Irrespective of the adopted RBE technique, a sensor trajectory planner is required to incorporate the new information, captured through new observations, into the planning process in order to make optimal decisions. Therefore, a cost function based on the target belief state is considered in information-theoretic applications to include all of the observations thus far in the planning process. Then the planner can be designed to make optimal decisions for one- or multi-step look-ahead depending on the planning horizon. Finally, this whole probabilistic search process can be formulated as a partially observed Markov decision process (POMDP) as in Aydemir et al. ( 2013 ), Hollinger et al. ( 2009 ), Martinez-Cantin et al. ( 2009 ) that incorporates all of the aforementioned steps. Here we discuss the related literature of various choices for cost functions and the planning horizon for LTS to better understand the potential options for an efficient online high dimensional LTS algorithm for a moving target in 3D. 2.1 Cost functions There are multiple choices for cost functions in the literature for search and exploration purposes. In a few examples, the trace or determinant of the covariance matrix of a set of estimated states is used. For instance in Zhou et al. ( 2008 ), the trace of the covariance matrix of the target’s position estimate at each time step is considered as the cost function for target localization. The same cost function, calculated over the entire planning horizon, is used in Kobilarov et al. ( 2011 ). For a similar application of online sensing and planning (Martinez-Cantin et al. 2009 ), the trace of the covariance matrix of the estimated state (robot pose and location of map features) at the end of a planning horizon forms the cost function. A popular choice of cost function in the field of LTS maximizes probability of detection, or in other words, minimizes the probability of no detection. The probability of detecting a target marginalized over all possible target states is maximized as a cost function in Bourgault et al. ( 2004 ), Lavis et al. ( 2008 ), Ma et al. ( 2011 ), Radmard and Croft ( 2011 ). Detecting a target for the first time at each iteration while considering no-detection likelihood in the previous iterations is used as a cost function in Tisdale et al. ( 2009 ), Geyer ( 2008 ). The cumulative sum of the probability of detecting the target for the first time, or cumulative detection probability (CDP), is also used in Lin et al. ( 2014 ), Niedfeldt et al. ( 2010 ), Lau et al. ( 2006 ). The incorporation of some additional terms like the inverse of travel distance along with the probability of detection in the cost function is also reported in Shubina and Tsotsos ( 2010 ), Espinoza et al. ( 2011 ), Webb and Furukawa ( 2008 ). Another popular cost function, based on information theory, maximizes the information gathered over a set of actions by minimizing the belief state entropy. There are numerous applications of this objective function in the field of robotics. For example, in a robot localization application using an adaptive particle filter approach, the number of particles is adapted based on the entropy of the robot’s state with respect to its environment (Cen et al. 2009 ). In Ramirez et al. ( 2014 ), for a target search application, the authors utilize a target state entropy cost function that considers an additional travel distance term to compensate for the scan area while planning ahead one step at a time. In Ryan and Hedrick ( 2010 ) a novel approach is presented to predict the expected target belief state entropy in an active sensing problem that drives a planner over a horizon. The authors show that a controller based on the maximum probability of detection resembles the outcome of a minimum entropy controller when the probability of detection is low. Following on this statement, we compare the performance of the two most common cost functions—probability of detection and belief state entropy—while considering additional terms to account for the sensor action’s cost. We present the result of this comparison both in simulation and on our platform by considering a greedy (one-step look-ahead) planning approach. The most promising cost function is then used to decide on the planning horizon when considering various horizon planning techniques in the literature. These are presented next. 2.2 Planning horizon In the target search and localization literature, sensor planning is applied in multi-step look-ahead fashion or simplified to one-step look-ahead, also known as greedy or myopic planning. Multi-step look-ahead planning is a well-known approach in control theory, known as model predictive control (MPC) or receding horizon control (RHC) (Mattingley et al. 2010 ). An optimization problem is solved at each time step to find an optimal series of actions while planning over a time horizon. Then the control input at that time step is the first action in that series of optimal actions. This optimization problem allows consideration of the control limitations and system constraints. However, solving this optimization problem for continuous nonlinear systems with high degrees of uncertainty in higher dimensions is prohibitive (Sommerlade 2010 ). RHC is an NP-hard problem as dimensions grow exponentially with planning horizon, and the only solution is to discretize the problem and turn the optimization into a combinatorial optimization problem. Various methods to discretize the target state space, the sensor’s control action, or a combination of both are proposed and compared in the literature. For example, grid-based planning is used in Lavis et al. ( 2008 ), Tisdale et al. ( 2009 ) to solve the unconstrained optimization for RHC of UAVs. A fixed five-step horizon planning is used in Lavis et al. ( 2008 ), while Tisdale et al. ( 2009 ) uses a variable horizon planning technique to decrease the dimensionality of the optimization as the sensor approaches the target. In the latter, when the sensor is far from the target, the planning horizon is increased, and as it approaches the target, short-term plans are made using a gradient-based optimizer. Another method to turn the optimization into a combinatorial problem is by discretizing the control action. This approach is applied to mobile robots and UAVs in Hollinger et al. ( 2009 ), Niedfeldt et al. ( 2010 ), Tian et al. ( 2008 ). In Hollinger et al. ( 2009 ) and Niedfeldt et al. ( 2010 ), a limited step look-ahead planning algorithm is considered due to the exponential nature of the RHC. For a set of \(\Psi \) discretized control actions in one step of a \(\tau \) -step horizon, \(\Psi ^{\tau }\) possible paths exist. In order to reduce this number, a rollout policy approach is proposed in Tian et al. ( 2008 ) that reduces the considered possible paths from \(\Psi ^{\tau }\) to \(\Psi +\left( {\tau -1} \right) \Psi ^{2}\) . This heuristic approach initiates a greedy search from the next step at any given time while marginalizing over all possible discretized actions at that time. This rollout policy for horizon planning of a UAV with a restricted motion model is claimed to guarantee its navigation safety in a hostile environment. Another popular approach to solve RHC is based on dynamic programming. The search for multiple stationary targets in a static environment is solved using dynamic programming in Lau et al. ( 2005 ), while dividing the environment into a set of distinct regions. In Geyer ( 2008 ) the authors adopt a dynamic programming principle to plan over a horizon for UAVs with a discrete set of control actions. They represent the target belief state by particle filter and compare the cost of different branches of control actions across search trees. Branch and bound techniques are also used for finite horizon planning in information gathering and object search objectives. The optimal search problem for a target in an indoor environment is solved by branch and bound in Lau et al. ( 2006 ) and Cabanillas et al. ( 2010 ). Informative path planning in an outdoor environment uses branch and bound in Binney and Sukhatme ( 2012 ) to speed up the planning while relying on a monotonic cost function of variance reduction. In all of these solutions based on branch and bound, the agent’s control action space is discretized in various forms to generate nodes for the search tree. Random trees provide the base for another horizon planning approach. The agent’s action is planned based on rapidly exploring random trees (RRT) in Kobilarov et al. ( 2011 ) while the target state estimation is evolved using a particle filter. The authors introduce shuffling and randomized pruning—techniques adopted from genetic algorithms—to improve computational efficiency of a random tree where the tree nodes are generated by biased sampling. The overall computational cost of this approach is \(\tau \Psi ^{2}\) . Although they were able to solve a search problem in 3D using random tree, the results are based on offline global open-loop optimization rather than in an online receding horizon fashion. Further improvements are provided in Hollinger and Sukhatme ( 2014 ) to consider various information objective alternatives for an informative motion planning problem. The incremental sample-based approach of RRT is also compared against rapidly exploring random graphs (RRG) and probabilistic roadmaps (PRM). However, generating trajectories in higher dimensions and in an online fashion are mentioned as future work that is another motivation for our work. When a sensor’s action and state space are high dimensional, discretization to solve RHC fails due to the curse of dimensionality. To handle this, multiple Monte Carlo methods are proposed in the literature to approximate the cost function over a horizon. In Martinez-Cantin et al. ( 2009 ) a state variance cost function is approximated by samples over a horizon of \(\tau \) while Bayesian optimization is used to minimize the number of queries to find the optimal path (policy). Linear interpolation of samples is also used in Ryan and Hedrick ( 2010 ) to approximate an entropy-based cost function over a horizon of \(\tau \) . However, approximating any cost function over a planning horizon with samples is a costly process, as it requires calculation of the posterior distribution for each of the samples. Computational cost is a major drawback of all the aforementioned approximation techniques to solve RHC. In order to avoid the computational cost for an online planning problem, the tendency in the literature is to adopt the simplified greedy planning approximation (Sommerlade 2010 ). The greedy planning approach is implemented on UAVs in Bertuccelli and How ( 2006 ), Chung and Burdick ( 2012 ), Bourgault et al. ( 2004 ), Ramirez et al. ( 2014 ), Wong et al. ( 2005 ), on mobile robots in Shubina and Tsotsos ( 2010 ), Zhou et al. ( 2008 ), Vander Hook et al. ( 2014 ), and on manipulators in Espinoza et al. ( 2011 ), Webb and Furukawa ( 2008 ), Radmard and Croft ( 2011 ) to search for a target. However, a greedy approach can get stuck in local minima. To overcome this problem, various heuristic methods are built upon greedy planning in Lin et al. ( 2014 ), Ma et al. ( 2011 ), Aydemir et al. ( 2013 ). We also propose a heuristic greedy approach to avoid getting stuck in local minima and compare its performance against a fast RHC method adopted from literature. Based on this review, in this work we present a particle filter approach to evolve the belief state of a target in 3D after leaving the camera FoV. Then an entropy-based cost function is proposed considering sensory platform constraints. This cost function is utilized in a heuristic greedy planner to drive an online optimization for a high dimensional robotic platform to efficiently search for the lost target. Each of these major components is compared against common methods in the literature, and both simulation and experimental results on an eye-in-hand system are presented to show the superior performance of our proposed algorithm. 3 Problem formulation When a moving target is inside the camera FoV, the target can be visually tracked and followed (visual tracking/servoing). Herein, we refer to this situation as the “tracking phase”. To improve the tracking performance, state estimation techniques can be applied to estimate the target motion based on some pre-assumed motion models (Tsai et al. 2009 ). When the target leaves the FoV, no new data about its state is available to the sensor, and tracking/servoing algorithms fail. However, the knowledge about the target based on the last available information prior to leaving the FoV can be used in an RBE to estimate its state over time. We refer to this situation as the “searching phase”. We assume that the appearance of the lost target remains invariant in 3D during the searching phase. This is a reasonable assumption as the target has just recently left the FoV and rapid recovery of the target is expected. This assumption in conjunction with availability of a recent history of the lost target are the two major assumptions that distinguish the LTS problem from a general search problem. If the target appearance changes during the searching phase, then viewpoint selection needs to be addressed, similar to the works in Wilkes and Tsotsos ( 1992 ), Dickinson et al. ( 1994 ), Torabi and Gupta ( 2012 ) for tracking and modeling applications. Also, semantic information about the target and the environment needs to be considered—as in Lin et al. ( 2014 ) and Aydemir et al. ( 2013 )—to capture the target’s a priori information if a recent history of the lost target is not available. The state transition model of the target irrespective of its situation with respect to the FoV is represented by: $$\begin{aligned} {\varvec{x}}_{k+1} =f_{k+1} \left( {{\varvec{x}}_k ,{\varvec{w}}_k } \right) , \end{aligned}$$ (1) or in the probabilistic form, \(p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{x}}_k } \right) \) , also known as process model. Here \({\varvec{x}}_k \in R^{n_x }\) is the target state at time step k and \(f_{k+1} :R^{n_x }\times R^{n_w }\rightarrow R^{n_x }\) is any possible function of the target motion. The independent and identically distributed (i.i.d) process noise, \({\varvec{w}}_k \) , represents the uncertainty in target state. Herein, the dimension of a vector \({\varvec{\alpha }}\) is written as \(n_\alpha \) . The sensor motion model is given by: $$\begin{aligned} {\varvec{q}}_{k+1} =g_{k+1} \left( {{\varvec{q}}_k ,{\varvec{u}}_k } \right) , \end{aligned}$$ (2) where \({\varvec{q}}_k \in R^{n_q }\) is the sensor state at time step k and \(g_{k+1} :R^{n_q }\times R^{n_u }\rightarrow R^{n_q }\) is any possible function of the sensor motion given its control input \({\varvec{u}}_k \) . Here we assume perfect sensor localization, which in turn assumes a perfect robot model. This is a valid assumption as any uncertainty in the robot model can be captured in the observation model noise term. The mounted camera on the robot provides this observation model: $$\begin{aligned} {\varvec{z}}_k =h_k \left( {{\varvec{x}}_k ,{\varvec{q}}_k ,{\varvec{v}}_k } \right) , \end{aligned}$$ (3) or in the probabilistic form, \(p\left( {{\varvec{z}}_{k} |{\varvec{x}}_{k} ,{\varvec{q}}_{k} } \right) \) , also known as likelihood. Here \({\varvec{z}}_{k} \) is the observation at time step k and \(h_k :R^{n_x }\times R^{n_q }\times R^{n_v }\rightarrow R^{n_z }\) is any possible observation function considering i.i.d measurement noise \({\varvec{v}}_k \) . Given a series of observations \({\varvec{z}}_{1:k} =\left\{ {{\varvec{z}}_i ,i=1, \ldots , k} \right\} \) , RBE calculates the target belief state—or the posterior distribution of \(p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_k |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k} } \right) \) —recursively through a prediction stage and an update stage. The prediction stage combines a priori information with the state transition model through the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation as follows: $$\begin{aligned} p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k} } \right) =\smallint p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{x}}_k } \right) p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_k |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k} } \right) d{\varvec{x}}_k . \end{aligned}$$ (4) As new observations become available, Bayes’ rule updates the target belief state as follows: $$\begin{aligned} p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k+1} } \right) =\frac{p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k} } \right) p\left( {{\varvec{z}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} ,{\varvec{q}}_{k+1} } \right) }{\smallint p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k} } \right) p\left( {{\varvec{z}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} ,{\varvec{q}}_{k+1} } \right) d{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} }.\nonumber \\ \end{aligned}$$ (5) The process starts with ( 4 ) from \(p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_1 |{\varvec{z}}_{1:1} } \right) \equiv p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_0 } \right) \) , where \({\varvec{z}}_{1:1} \) is the last available observation from the tracking phase. The goal is to generate an optimal or sub-optimal sensor control input \({\varvec{u}}_k \) in order to find the lost target as soon as possible with minimum cost. Therefore, a cost function based on target state and as a function of sensor control input is formed. Depending on how the cost function is formed, a single control input \({\varvec{u}}_k \) or a sequence of control inputs \({\varvec{u}}_{k:k+\tau } \) over a horizon \(\tau \) is planned at time step k , though only \({\varvec{u}}_k \) is applied at each step to acquire a new observation. Then ( 5 ) is used to update the target belief state. This whole process iterates until the target is recovered and the searching phase is terminated. Fig. 2 Full size image a A scaled detection model in \(\mathcal{F}_{i} \) for a specific depth with depth coefficient \({{\varvec{\Gamma }} }\left( {d_{k}} \right) =\mathbf{1}\) . b Schematic representation of the detection model 4 Methodology In order to plan efficient sensor actions, the first step is to solve the RBE to evolve the target belief state. To handle the nonlinearities in both the observation model and some state transition models as well as any noise term, we apply the Sampling Importance Resampling (SIR) particle filter with residual resampling step as detailed in Doucet et al. ( 2001 ), Arulampalam et al. ( 2002 ). In this case, target’s a priori distribution \( p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_k |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k} } \right) \) starting from \(p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_0 } \right) \) is approximated by a set of N weighted Dirac delta functions as follows: $$\begin{aligned} p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_k |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k} } \right) \approx {\mathop {\sum }\limits _{i=1}^{N}} \omega _k^i \delta \left( {{\varvec{x}}_k -{\varvec{x}}_k^i } \right) , \end{aligned}$$ (6) where \({\varvec{x}}_k^i \) is one of the N particles. The particles transit through the state transition model in SIR, while their normalized associated weights, \(\left\{ {\omega _k^i ,i=1:N} \right\} \) , are calculated based on the principle of importance sampling through the observation model and the resampling stage such that \({\mathop {\sum }\nolimits _{i=1}^{N}} \omega _k^i =1\) . In this work, the particles are points ( \({\varvec{x}}_k^i =\left[ {x,y,z} \right] _k^i )\) in Cartesian space representing target location probability, as \({\varvec{x}}\) is expressed in Cartesian coordinates ( \(n_x =3)\) . We also express our sensor state in the robot configuration space (typically a 6 or 7 dimensional space for a robotic manipulator) where the vector \({\varvec{q}}\) represents a joint angle vector ( \({\varvec{q}}_k =\left[ {q_1 ,\ldots ,q_{n_q } } \right] _k )\) . By choosing to plan sensor actions in the configuration space, we both avoid the need for robot singularity handling and feasibility checks through inverse kinematics (IK), and also facilitate joint limit avoidance by implementing them as constraints. Therefore, the observation model in ( 3 ) must bridge between these two spaces in order to update the belief state by particle filter. 4.1 Observation model The whole process of search and tracking can be handled through a particle filter as long as a thorough observation model is available. During the tracking phase a detection event represents the observation, \({\varvec{z}}_k ={\varvec{D}}_k \) , while during the searching phase, a no-detection event represents the worst-case observation scenario by considering \({\varvec{z}}_k ={\bar{{\varvec{D}}}}_k \) . As the observations in the tracking and searching phase are complementary, the detection and no-detection models are combined in a Bernoulli model to cover both cases as follows: $$\begin{aligned}&p\left( {{\varvec{z}}_k |{\varvec{x}}_k ,{\varvec{q}}_k } \right) =\left[ {p\left( {{\varvec{D}}_k |{\varvec{x}}_k ,{\varvec{q}}_k } \right) } \right] ^{I_1 \left( {{\varvec{x}}_k ,{\varvec{q}}_k } \right) }.\nonumber \\&\quad \left[ {p\left( {{\bar{{\varvec{D}}}}_k |{\varvec{x}}_k ,{\varvec{q}}_k } \right) } \right] ^{I_2 \left( {{\varvec{x}}_k ,{\varvec{q}}_k } \right) }, I_i =0~or~ 1. \end{aligned}$$ (7) Here we focus on the detection model, since the no-detection model used in the searching phase is simply the complementary part. Our proposed continuous detection model, based on a pin-hole camera model, also encapsulates the depth parameter. A similar approach is considered in Ye and Tsotsos ( 1999 ) to generate a discrete geometry-based camera observation model called sensed sphere. This sensed sphere is a subsection of a sphere generated by intersecting camera view volume—defined by camera bounding planes—and concentric spheres of different radiuses centered at camera location. The observation probability is considered constant within this discretized sensed sphere which does not address both depth and boundary effects in image processing, and also can be computationally expensive to implement. To both consider the image edge effect and a nominal detection depth range as well as to bridge between the Cartesian space and configuration space, we define our heuristic detection model in the image frame, \(\mathcal{F}_i \) , considering \(\left[ {u,v,d} \right] _k \) coordinates. The image plane is a rectangular plane with poor detection capability on the edges. To encapsulate these features, we construct our detection model from the product of four sigmoid functions centered close to the edges of the image plane. When \(L_u \) and \(L_v \) represent the width in pixels of the image plane in u and v directions with a safety bound of size \(\epsilon \) , the model is expressed as follows: $$\begin{aligned}&p\left( {{\varvec{D}}_k |\left[ {u,v,d} \right] _k } \right) = \Gamma \left( {d_k } \right) .\nonumber \\&\quad {\mathop {\prod }\limits _{i=1}^{2}} \frac{1}{1+e^{\left( {-1} \right) ^{i}\alpha \left( {u_k -l_u } \right) }}.\frac{1}{1+e^{\left( {-1} \right) ^{i}\alpha \left( {v_k -l_v } \right) }},\nonumber \\&\quad l_u =\left( {-1} \right) ^{i}\left( {L_u /2-\epsilon } \right) , \nonumber \\&\quad l_{v} =\left( {-1} \right) ^{i}\left( {L_v /2-\epsilon } \right) , \end{aligned}$$ (8) where \(\alpha \) defines the slope of the sigmoid functions. In this model, \(\Gamma \left( {d_k } \right) \) is the depth coefficient to account for the depth effect in the detection model. This model is depicted in Fig. 2 a for the case when \(\Gamma \left( {d_k } \right) =1\) . The schematic representation of the overall detection model is presented in Fig. 2 b when the depth coefficient is defined as follows: $$\begin{aligned} \Gamma \left( {d_k } \right) =\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta _s \left( {d_k -d_s } \right) }}.\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta _l \left( {d_k -d_l } \right) }}. \end{aligned}$$ (9) This coefficient is formed by the product of two sigmoid functions of depth. The first function with a sharp slope ( \(\beta _{s})\) represents the shortest range, \(d_s \) , that the object is detectable by the camera. This shortest range is usually the depth at which the entire target fits in the FoV or the target image deblurs. The second function with a mild slope ( \(\beta _{l})\) defines the longest detection range, \(d_{l}\) , at which the target is so far away that is no longer detectable. Both \(d_{s}\) and \(d_{l}\) depend on the target size, sensor specifications, lighting condition and the detection algorithm that can be calculated or experimentally examined for a given platform in model base detection algorithms. In the case of a 3D camera like the Microsoft Kinect, where the depth information is extracted from point cloud data, this information is provided by the sensor’s manufacturer. The model presented in ( 8 ) is defined in the image space, while the particles representing the target location probability are expressed in the Cartesian space. To transfer all of the particles defined in the world frame to the image frame, we consider the robot base frame, \(\mathcal{F}_b \) , as the world frame. In this case all of the points are expressed through homogenous transformation matrices \({ }_o^b \mathbf{T}\) . Here, \({ }_o^b \mathbf{T}\) , \({ }_e^b \mathbf{T}\) and \({ }_c^e \mathbf{T}\) represent transformation matrices from the object frame to the base frame, the robot end-effector frame to the base frame and the camera frame to the end-effector frame respectively. Thus, any object can be expressed in the camera frame, \({ }_o^c \mathbf{T}\) , by the following kinematic transformation: $$\begin{aligned} { }_o^c \mathbf{T}={ }_c^e \mathbf{T}^{-1}{ }_e^b \mathbf{T}^{-1}\left( {q_k } \right) { }_o^b \mathbf{T}. \end{aligned}$$ (10) Here, \({ }_c^e \mathbf{T}\) is the camera extrinsic parameters matrix captured through camera calibration, while \({ }_e^b \mathbf{T}\) is a function of the sensor state \({\varvec{q}}_k \) . Note that \({ }_e^b \mathbf{T}\) is calculated through the robot’s forward kinematics and accounts for the sensor motion model in ( 2 ) affecting the overall observation model. Therefore, any point \({\varvec{x}}_k \) at time step k expressed in the base frame \(\mathcal{F}_b \) can be expressed in the image frame \(\mathcal{F}_i \) as a function of \({\varvec{q}}_k \) as follows: (11) Here, \(\hbox {K}\) is the camera calibration matrix defined by the camera intrinsic parameters, and \(\left[ {I|0} \right] \) is a matrix formed by a 3x3 identity matrix and a zero vector. Substituting ( 11 ) and ( 9 ) into ( 8 ) provides the heuristic detection model that is a function of the target and sensor states ( \({\varvec{x}}_k \) and \({\varvec{q}}_k )\) . This detection model or its complement is then used to update the target belief state in the particle filter update stage. It is also used to form different cost functions; by substituting ( 2 ) into ( 11 ), optimal sensor control actions can be derived to make efficient observations. In the next section we develop a cost function to make efficient sensor actions in the searching phase. Therefore, the no-detection model is considered as the observation model from now on unless stated otherwise. As the target is recovered, the same procedure can be followed by adopting the detection model instead to continue with the tracking phase. 4.2 Cost function The entropy of a random variable in information theory is a measure of its uncertainty. For a continuous random variable \(\theta \) with PDF \(p\left( \theta \right) \) , the entropy is calculated as follows: $$\begin{aligned} \hbox {H}\left( {p\left( \theta \right) } \right) =-\smallint p\left( \theta \right) \log p\left( \theta \right) d\theta . \end{aligned}$$ (12) As more information becomes available, the random variable entropy reduces. Therefore, the entropy of the target belief state can provide us with a cost function in order to make informative optimal observations. As observations are collected over time, the entropy is calculated as follows: $$\begin{aligned}&\hbox {H}\left( {p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k+1} } \right) } \right) \nonumber \\&\quad =-\smallint p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k+1} } \right) \log p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k+1} } \right) d{\varvec{x}}_k . \end{aligned}$$ (13) Utilizing the particle filter approximation, one can substitute ( 6 ) into ( 13 ) to achieve the following approximation: $$\begin{aligned} \hbox {H}\left( {p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k+1} } \right) } \right) \approx -{\mathop {\sum }\limits _{i=1}^{N}} \omega _{k+1}^i \log \omega _{k+1}^i , \end{aligned}$$ (14) which is the same as the entropy of a discrete random variable. Even though the particles’ locations are not addressed in this approximation, this is a sufficient measure to reduce uncertainty of the target belief state based on the particles’ weights, as long as the weights are updated through observations. The case to account for particles’ location when the weights are not updated will be handled later when we propose our greedy planning. We also consider an additional travel distance term in the cost function to minimize the sensor action cost. This addresses the fact that, in high-DoF robotic platforms, multiple robot configurations can result in the same end-effector location, or that different observations might result in similar entropy reductions. The resulting cost function is then as follows: $$\begin{aligned} \hbox {J}\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} ,{\varvec{q}}_k ,{\varvec{q}}_{k+1} } \right)= & {} -\left( {1-\rho } \right) {\mathop {\sum } \limits _{i=1}^{N}} \omega _{k+1}^i \log \omega _{k+1}^{i}\nonumber \\&+\,\rho \left( {{\varvec{q}}_k -{\varvec{q}}_{k+1} } \right) ^{\mathrm{T}}\Phi \left( {{\varvec{q}}_k -{\varvec{q}}_{k+1} } \right) .\nonumber \\ \end{aligned}$$ (15) Here \(0<\rho \le 1\) is a coefficient to adjust the emphasis on travel distance, while \(\Phi \) is a diagonal matrix assigning different positive weights to different robot joints, as they may not have equivalent travel costs. This cost function is similar to the one presented in Ramirez et al. ( 2014 ); however, we will further improve it later to account for sensor constraints and particle locations. We will also compare the performance of this cost function against the probability of detection cost function. The probability of detection cost function that we chose for comparison is the probability of no-detection when it is marginalized over all possible target states. This is the same as the normalization factor in the update formula in ( 5 ) for no-detection likelihood, and it represents the volume under the target belief state PDF after being carved by the no-detection likelihood. Considering the normalization factor in ( 5 ) in its particle filter representation and adding the travel distance term provides us with an alternative cost function as follows, similar to the one in Espinoza et al. ( 2011 ): $$\begin{aligned} \hbox {J}\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} ,{\varvec{q}}_k ,{\varvec{q}}_{k+1} } \right)= & {} \left( {1-\rho } \right) {\mathop {\sum }\limits _{i=1}^{N}} \omega _{k}^{i} p\left( {{\varvec{z}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{x}}_{k+1}^{i} ,{\varvec{q}}_{k+1}} \right) \nonumber \\&+\,\rho \left( {{\varvec{q}}_k -{\varvec{q}}_{k+1} } \right) ^{\mathrm{T}}\Phi \left( {{\varvec{q}}_k -{\varvec{q}}_{k+1}} \right) .\nonumber \\ \end{aligned}$$ (16) Irrespective of the adopted cost function, the planner should be able to account for the robot motion limitations such as joint and velocity limits via constraints. These limitations and constraints are discussed in the following section. 4.3 Constraints The constraints applied in the literature are usually of simple form, such as maximum turning rate or speed in UAV and mobile robots, that are implemented in the sensor motion model. However, in the case of high-DoF robotic manipulators with nonlinear constraints of joint limits, a constraint optimization is required. To avoid complexity, we introduce a penalty function to our cost functions in ( 15 ) and ( 16 ) in the following form in order to account for the joint limits: $$\begin{aligned}&\Lambda \left( {{\varvec{q}}_k } \right) ={\mathop {\prod }\limits _{i=1}^{n_q}} \left[ {\lambda \left( {q_i } \right) } \right] _{k},\nonumber \\&\left[ {\lambda \left( {q_i } \right) } \right] _k \!=\!\left( {1+e^{-\gamma \frac{\left( {q_i -q_{imin} } \right) \left( {q_{imax} -q_i } \right) }{\left( {q_{imax} -q_{imin} } \right) ^{2}}}} \right) _k , \quad i\!=\!1,\ldots ,n_q .\nonumber \\ \end{aligned}$$ (17) In this penalty function, \(q_{imin} \) and \(q_{imax} \) represent the minimum and maximum joint limits of joint i respectively. The coefficient \(\gamma \) is chosen to be a large number such that it will suddenly increase the cost close to the joint limits. This penalty function is depicted in Fig. 3 for a sample joint with joint limits of \(\left[ {-3~3} \right] ~rad\) and various \(\gamma \) . This penalty function is applied to the cost functions in ( 15 ) and ( 16 ) as follows: $$\begin{aligned} \hbox {J}_c \left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} ,{\varvec{q}}_k ,{\varvec{q}}_{k+1} } \right) = \Lambda \left( {{\varvec{q}}_k } \right) .\hbox {J}\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} ,{\varvec{q}}_k ,{\varvec{q}}_{k+1} } \right) . \end{aligned}$$ (18) Fig. 3 Full size image An example of the penalty function for a joint with joint limits of \(\left[ {\mathbf{- 3\,\, 3}} \right] ~ rad\) The same approach can be adopted to take the velocity and acceleration limits of a robotic manipulator into account. However, an alternative approach is to implement these limits in the sensor motion model in ( 2 ). In this work we implement the velocity limits as the constraints in the sensor motion model, as we manipulate our hardware through velocity control. This is provided by: $$\begin{aligned} {\varvec{q}}_{k+1} ={\varvec{q}}_k +{\varvec{u}}_k \Delta , \quad 0<\left| {{\varvec{u}}_k } \right| <{\dot{\varvec{q}}}_{max}, \end{aligned}$$ (19) where \(\Delta \) is the time step in the control loop and \(\ddot{{\varvec{q}}}_{max}\) is the robot maximum velocity vector in joint space. Substituting this equation into ( 11 ) provides us with an observation model as a function of robot velocity. This allows us to plan observations in a greedy or RHC fashion, as discussed in the next section. 4.4 Planning horizon Even though the low computational cost of greedy planning is appealing for a fast and efficient online LTS, it has the potential to get stuck in local minima (Lin et al. 2014 ). As indicated in the literature, RHC can not only ameliorate this issue; it also has other benefits in this context. For example, the navigational challenges of a UAV in a hostile environment makes RHC inevitable, as discussed in Tian et al. ( 2008 ). RHC is also helpful for cases where sensors have limited FoV or constrained footprint (Ryan and Hedrick 2010 ; Tisdale et al. 2009 ). In such systems, if the sensor location is far from the prior target distribution, a greedy search would not return an optimal solution. This is the case where the particles’ weights do not get updated, as mentioned earlier in the cost function section. This is a rare scenario in UAV search, as usually a broad prior PDF covers the entire search space based on the map, the available information of the terrain, and the lost target (Lin et al. 2014 ). In eye-in-hand systems, even if the robot has limited navigational challenges apart from joint limits, the mounted camera is a limited FoV sensor, and the lost target has narrow prior PDF based on the limited information prior to leaving the FoV. Therefore RHC is an inevitable choice, unless we address the case where the next step observation over the entire range of sensor motion does not return any information. In such a case, as depicted in Fig. 4 b, for a simplified 2D search the cost of any action is similar, and the system lacks a policy to make an action. Instead of horizon planning for such scenario, we propose a switching objective function to solve this challenge for greedy planning. Whenever the observation model does not return any new information, we switch from minimizing the belief state entropy (or probability of no-detection) in ( 18 ), to minimizing the distance between the agent and the observation location that maximizes information gain. The switching happens when the observation model does not carve out any volume from the target belief state PDF. This switch is implemented through the detection model in ( 8 ) when the observed weights of all particles for detection event return close to zero. Considering the particles’ weight vector, \({\varvec{\omega }} _k \) , and a small number \(\varepsilon \) , the switch is: $$\begin{aligned} ||{\varvec{\omega }} _k ||<\varepsilon ,\quad when \quad {\varvec{z}}_k ={\varvec{D}}_k . \end{aligned}$$ (20) When the switch happens, we propose to apply a sample-based approach that is more aligned with information gain. In fact we try to sample from \(p\left( {{\varvec{z}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k} } \right) \) when \({\varvec{z}}_k ={\varvec{D}}_k \) , that is, the future observation in one step based on all previous observations. This is the PDF that appears in the denominator of the update formula in ( 5 ) for detection likelihood: $$\begin{aligned} p\left( {{\varvec{z}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k} } \right) \!=\!{\smallint } p\left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k} } \right) p\left( {{\varvec{z}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} ,{\varvec{q}}_{k+1} } \right) d{\varvec{x}}_{k+1} .\nonumber \\ \end{aligned}$$ (21) By sampling from this distribution, we are simulating a combinatorial optimization technique to find an approximate optimal sensor state \({\hat{{\varvec{q}}}}_{k+1} \) , which in turn minimizes the belief state entropy in the next step so long as the observation could happen at that state. Then we try to minimize the distance from the current sensor state \({\varvec{q}}_k \) to that configuration as it is not possible to jump from \({\varvec{q}}_k \) to \(\hat{{{\varvec{q}}}}_{k+1} \) in one step. This generates an action towards an optimal location in the future when no new sensor action in one step from \({\varvec{q}}_k \) can return new information. The distribution in ( 21 ) is formed by the propagated target state through the state transition model and the detection model. We follow the approach presented in Ryan and Hedrick ( 2010 ) to randomly sample from this distribution when the target PDF is approximated by the particle filter. The particle filter allows us to use the particle locations and weights to approximate the distribution in ( 21 ) as follows: $$\begin{aligned} p\left( {{\varvec{z}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k} } \right) ={\mathop {\sum }\limits _{i=1}^{N}} {\omega _{k}^{i}} p\left( {{\varvec{z}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{x}}_{k+1}^i } \right) . \end{aligned}$$ (22) In order to facilitate the random sampling process from ( 22 ), we need to replace the detection model with a distribution from which random sampling techniques already exist. A normal distribution in the particle’s space (Cartesian space) with mean, \(\mu ,\) and covariance, \(\sum \) , as functions of \({\varvec{x}}_{k+1}^i \) is a reasonable assumption to replace the detection model (achieved by substituting ( 11 ) into ( 8 )). The resulting multivariate Gaussian mixture model (GMM) is as follows: $$\begin{aligned} p\left( {{\varvec{z}}_{k+1} |{\varvec{z}}_{1:k} } \right) =\mathop \sum \limits _{i=1}^N \omega _k^i \mathcal{N}\left( {\mu \left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1}^i } \right) ,\sum \left( {{\varvec{x}}_{k+1}^i } \right) } \right) . \end{aligned}$$ (23) We sample \(N_s \) times ( \({\varvec{x}}_{k+1}^s , s=1:N_s )\) from this distribution, where our number of samples is much smaller than our number of particles ( \(N_s <N)\) . The number of samples is chosen by trial and error through an iterative process as explained in Ryan and Hedrick ( 2010 ). Then each sample in Cartesian space is simply associated with a camera transformation matrix, \({ }_c^b \mathbf{T}_{k+1}^s \) , based on geometry by assuming that only camera orientation should change to point at the sample position. This is a valid assumption, as long as the target is not lost due to an occlusion. These camera transformation matrices, \(\left\{ {{ }_c^b {\mathbf{T}_{k+1}^{s}}{ } ^{-1},s=1:N_s } \right\} \) , replace \({ }_c^e \mathbf{T}^{-1}{ }_e^b \mathbf{T}^{-1}\left( {{\varvec{q}}_k } \right) \) in ( 11 ) to provide simulated sample observations based on our heuristic observation model. Then the sample observation that gains maximum information (minimum entropy) results in an optimal sample point \(\hat{{x}}_{k+1} \) . $$\begin{aligned} {\hat{{\varvec{x}}}}_{k+1} ={\mathop {\hbox {argmin}}\limits _{x_{k+1}^s}} \left( -{\mathop {\sum }\limits _{i=1}^{N}} \omega _{k+1}^i \log \omega _{k+1}^{i} \right) ,~s=1:N_{s}. \end{aligned}$$ (24) Finally the \({\hat{{\varvec{x}}}}_{k+1} \) associated optimal camera transformation matrix, \({ }_c^b {\hat{\mathbf{T}}}_{k+1} \) , results in the optimal sensor state, \({\hat{q}}_{k+1} \) , through one call of IK. This approach allows us to have different target and sensor spaces while bridging between them with low computational cost. It is through this switching greedy approach that we account for particle locations in the cost function and provide a tool to avoid getting stuck in local minima. Our overall greedy LTS algorithm including the switching cost function is presented in Algorithm 1. The stage a) in Algorithm 1 uses a constrained nonlinear solver (fmincon in MATLAB) to generate optimal \({\varvec{u}}_k \) that minimizes ( 18 ). All of the stages are addressed in Algorithm 1 A similar approach to our switching cost function is proposed in Wong et al. ( 2005 ), where the objective function switches between the CDP, and the agent’s distance to the mode of the target PDF, when the CDP goes below a certain threshold. Their switch is the same as replacing the optimal sample in ( 24 ) with: $$\begin{aligned} {\hat{{\varvec{x}}}}_{k+1} ={\varvec{x}}_{k+1}^i ,\hbox { i}={\mathop {\hbox {argmax}}\limits _{i}} \left( {\omega _i } \right) . \end{aligned}$$ (25) This approach is interpreted as a tradeoff between detection reward along the path and the distance to the most probable place of the target. The same approach is adopted in Webb and Furukawa ( 2008 ) in the form of a multi objective function, where probability of detection and distance of the sensor from the mode of the target PDF are combined linearly. However, it is claimed that the detection objective tends to explore local areas with high probability before moving the sensor to the area of maximum probability. Navigating towards the peak of the target belief state PDF has also been applied as the only planner in the general search method in Ma et al. ( 2011 ) and is known as streaming Drosophila search (Chung and Burdick 2012 ). Another switching approach is used in Chung and Furukawa ( 2009 ) to plan greedy actions through a linear combination of a search and capture objective. When the sensor agent is far from the target, the distance between the agent and the weighted average of the particles (mean of the target belief state PDF) is minimized, and as it gets closer, the uncertainty in target state is minimized by minimizing the sum of variances on the state. This switch is the same as replacing the optimal sample in ( 24 ) with: $$\begin{aligned} {\hat{x}}_{k+1} ={\mathop {\sum }\limits _{i=1}^{N}} \omega _i x_{k+1}^{i} . \end{aligned}$$ (26) In the result section we demonstrate the benefits of using ( 24 ) instead of ( 25 ) and ( 26 ), while keeping the rest of the algorithm consistent. We believe that our approach is a more general approach as it can include both ( 25 ) and ( 26 ) as preselected samples for ( 24 ). We also compare the performance of our greedy planning using a switching cost function against a RHC method. We choose a computationally cheap method to approximate the cost function over a horizon. We simplify the information theoretic approach presented in Ryan and Hedrick ( 2010 ) as follows to reduce the computational cost and make it more suitable for an online LTS planning. Instead of constructing an approximate cost function from samples over a planning horizon \(\tau \) to plan the optimal control action, we choose the action associated with the sample with minimum cost as a worst-case (minimax) approach. We also simplify the entropy calculation to ( 14 ) instead of constructing a continuous distribution from particles for entropy measurement. Finally, we implement our proposed greedy planner to drive an efficient search on a high dimensional robotic platform. This implementation fully reveals the potential of our proposed observation model to update our knowledge of the target state in 3D Cartesian space when the sensor makes actions in 7D configuration space. It also shows rapid recovery of a lost target for visual servoing and tracking applications where online and efficient algorithms are required to keep the target in the FoV as much as possible. Treating the mounted camera as a pan-tilt search platform as a result simplifies our comparisons and makes them analogous to the most common 2D search in the UAV literature. We also believe that as long as there is no occlusion, a pan-tilt search is a feasible search for online robotic vision applications. Alternatively, a specific time is considered as another termination condition while CDPs are compared as a quality measure (Lin et al. 2014 ). Here we demonstrate the common drawback of each alternative option. The shortcoming of the switch presented in ( 25 ) is evident when all the particles have the same weight, e.g., at the beginning when no observation is made. In this case any particles can represent the mode of the target PDF as depicted in Fig. 5 a, delaying the information gathering process. The drawback of the switch in ( 26 ) is when there is a bifurcation in particle distribution, in which case the target PDF mean is a local minimum that no particle gets updated on, as depicted in Fig. 5 b. On the other hand, our proposed switch in ( 24 ) that always tries to gain maximum information is a more general switch, as it can include both of the alternative switches as two hardcoded samples. The only argument against our proposed switch could be its computational cost, which is a fraction of the entire algorithm as long as ( \(N_s <N)\) . Therefore, we proceed with our proposed switch from now on. Considering our proposed switch, we compare the cost functions in a pan-tilt search for a moving target and greedy planning. We keep the robot initial configuration fixed while generating 100 random constant acceleration motion models with random initial position and velocity for the target. We keep this repository of random target motions consistent in all of our simulation evaluations. This allows us to track the effects of cost function, horizon planning, and sensor dimensionality, as presented later. The simulation results for the two cost function choices are presented in Fig. 6 a. Note that the T-bars in Fig. 6 a, as well as in the upcoming figures, represent 95 % of the range of the measured parameter given multiple (100 in this case) random initial conditions. Even though the entropy-based cost function outperforms the probability of detection cost function from the time step point of view, its travel distance is only slightly better. Comparing within each trial, the entropy-based pan-tilt greedy search has fewer time steps in 55 % of the trials and less travel distance in 39 % of the trials than the probability of detection pan-tilt greedy search. This similar performance confirms the statement in Ryan and Hedrick ( 2010 ) that in low detection probability “the maximum probability of detection has been shown to nearly solve the minimum entropy problem.” We also compare the cost functions’ performances in real experiments. In order to provide consistent and challenging conditions for comparison, we designed an experiment in which the target is intentionally occluded for a certain period of time. We placed a tunnel (depicted in Fig. 7 ) over a section of the train track, making the target suddenly invisible during tracking for about 8.1 s (65 cm) while the train was moving at around 8 cm/s. While our visual servoing algorithm tracked the target using all of the 7 DoFs, a pan-tilt search was triggered as soon as the target went into the tunnel. This experiment was conducted 40 times for each cost function, and their results are presented in Fig. 6 b. Among 40 experimental trials for each of the cost functions, 15 % of them failed and were excluded from the results in Fig. 6 b. We defined a trial failure as when the train finishes a lap without being recovered by the robot, i.e., the target would have been recovered if the robot just had waited for it to show up again without searching. However, all of the failed trials did recover the target in the train’s second lap (after the train came out of the tunnel again). Apart from the number of time steps and travel distance, we compared the actual time it took the planner to recover the target from the moment that the target left the FoV including the 8.1 s it spent under the tunnel. This comparison indicates that, even in a greedy searching phase, our robot control loop rate slowed down to about 8 Hz consistently for both cost functions, compared to about 16 Hz in the tracking phase. Finally, the experimental results presented in Fig. 6 b confirm the simulation results, namely, that both cost functions perform similarly from all measures point of view. However, we choose the entropy-based cost function for our LTS algorithm upon its slightly better performance for further evaluation in the following sections. Fig. 8 Full size image Greedy planning versus RHC from time step and travel distance point of view for 100 simulation trials Fig. 9 Full size image 2-DoF (pan-tilt) search versus 7-DoF (high dimensional) search, a from time step and travel distance point of view for 100 simulation trials, b from time step, time and travel distance point of view for 20 experimental trials 5.3 Planning horizon To compare the performance of our proposed greedy algorithm against a RHC, we consider a pan-tilt search in simulation only with the same initial conditions as last section. The results of our entropy-based pan-tilt greedy search presented in the last section (Fig. 6 a) is compared against a computationally cheap RHC with horizon planning of 5 and 40 trajectory samples, as explained previously. The results, as presented in Fig. 8 , show the superior performance of our proposed greedy algorithm for both measures. Comparing within each trial, the entropy-based pan-tilt greedy search has fewer time steps in 89 % of the trials and less travel distance in 56 % of the trials than the entropy-based pan-tilt RHC. However, one cannot conclude that RHC has lower performance in LTS in general, as our presented RHC is highly simplified to satisfy low computational cost for online applications. We believe that a more sophisticated RHC, as presented in Ryan and Hedrick ( 2010 ), has the potential to beat the greedy performance, but its computational cost would make it intractable for online real world visual servoing/tracking implementation. This is supported by the fact that even our simplified RHC in its current format for pan-tilt search is still more expensive than our greedy approach, as computing the belief state for each of the trajectory samples is costly. The RHC computational cost is further discussed in the next section. 5.4 Generalizing to higher dimensions Another benefit of a greedy search is its capability to increase the problem dimensionality. Given our LTS problem, we can expand the search over all of the robot’s DoFs. However, in RHC this would add exponentially to the solution space and make it intractable. For example in Geyer ( 2008 ), replacing a UAV’s fixed camera with a gimbal camera is presented as a challenge, as it adds to the sensor space dimensions and requires more branching in the path optimization problem. Fig. 10 Full size image A sample experiment comparing 2-DoF (pan-tilt) search versus 7-DoF (high dimensional) search: a tracking phase, b the moment that the target leaves the camera FoV, c the moment that the target is recovered in a 2-DoF search, d the moment that the target is recovered in a 7-DoF search We have formulated our cost function in ( 18 ) in such a way that we can easily extend the sensor state to higher dimensions while considering all of the constraints. Therefore, we compare the performance of our pan-tilt search (Figs. 6 a and 8 ) against the full 7-DoF search in simulation while considering the same initial conditions. The results as presented in Fig. 9 a show that the 7-DoF search outperforms the pan-tilt search in both measures. Comparing within each trial also shows that our entropy-based 7-DoF greedy search has fewer time steps in 77 % of the trials and less travel distance in 89 % of the trials than the entropy-based pan-tilt greedy search. The main reason, as observed during simulation trials, is that the extra DoFs of the sensor allow it to zoom back quickly to reacquire the target. This action provides a broader FoV around the lost target vicinity resulting in a quicker recovery. To validate our simulation work, we compared the performance of our pan-tilt versus 7-DoF search in an experiment for the actual case where our visual servoing algorithm could not keep up with our faster moving target, as described in the experimental platform section. During 20 experimental trials for each of the search methods (pan-tilt versus 7-DoF), the robot lost the fast moving target at some point during tracking phase. At that point the LTS was triggered and remained active until the target was recovered. The results were compared from time step, time, and travel distance point of view as presented in Fig. 9 b. In these experimental results the 7-DoF search outperforms the pan-tilt search from time step and travel distance point of view confirming the simulation results. However, the actual target recovery time in 7-DoF search takes longer than pan-tilt search. This is related to the higher computational cost for the 7-DoF search compared to pan-tilt search, as these planners were controlling the robot on average at 3 and 5 Hz respectively during searching phase. The zooming back nature of the 7-DoF search actions to recover the target was usually observed during the experimental trials. A sample trial is depicted in Fig. 10 for both pan-tilt and 7-DoF search. The tracking phase of the experiment is shown in Fig. 10 a while the moment that the searching phase is triggered is shown in Fig. 10 b. The moment that the pan-tilt search recovers the target solely by reorienting the mounted camera is depicted in Fig. 10 c). For comparison, Fig. 10 d shows the target recovery moment by 7-DoF search where planned zooming back in addition to camera reorientation leads to target recovery. 6 Conclusion and future work In this work we discussed the problem of eye-in-hand systems as a high dimensional robotic platform actively recovering a target which leaves the camera FoV in an online manner. As robots move into more complex and unstructured environments, solving this LTS problem is highly valuable for visual tracking/servoing applications. It increases robot’s level of autonomy by providing a transition to recover a lost target as soon as possible with minimum cost. In this work we addressed this problem in a holistic manner and systematically compared it to other approaches in the literature. A novel observation model was presented to relate the space in which the target state evolves to the space in which the agent plans the search, since these two spaces can be of different types and dimensions. The problem was formulated as an optimization problem to plan fast and low cost sensor actions in higher dimensions while avoiding robot’s kinematic constraints. We believe that using the particle filter, our novel observation model, switching cost function, and greedy planning are the key steps towards online search in higher dimensions. The particle filter allows us to evolve the target belief state cheaply, suitable for higher dimensions of target space such as 3D Cartesian space. Our observation model provides the link between the target space and the robot configuration space. Our switching cost function avoids getting stuck in local minima while planning informative sensor actions considering robot’s kinematic constraints. The greedy planning approach also allows increasing the problem dimensionality with low computational cost, making it suitable for high dimensional online LTS. We then compared the performance of various features of our overall algorithm in simulation trials against other common methods to show the superior performance of our proposed algorithm. Among entropy-based pan-tilt greedy search, probability of detection pan-tilt greedy search, entropy-based pan-tilt RHC, and entropy-based 7-DoF greedy search, all of which were applied to the same 100 randomly generated simulation trials, our entropy-based 7-DoF greedy search outperformed all of the other methods. Comparing within each trial of all methods, it has fewer time steps in 72 % of the trials and less travel distance in 77 % of the trials. Real world implementation of the algorithm was also presented on an eye-in-hand visual servoing platform while servoing to and tracking a moving target. We demonstrated that our high dimensional online LTS is capable of recovering a fast moving target quickly after it leaves the FoV due to the limitations of our visual servoing algorithm. Our results also showed that a high dimensional search improves the performance, as extra DoFs provide better clearing actions. In addition, a pan-tilt search might not provide feasible views due to environmental constraints. For example, a lost target scenario caused by an occlusion cannot be handled through a pan-tilt search due to the physics of the scenario. In such scenarios, the high dimensional search should be used to reposition the camera in addition to potential reorientation through planning. Solving the optimization problem in the presence of occlusions is an interesting future work that is not addressed here. Apart from occlusions, other environmental constraints such as obstacles and self-collision are introduced challenges when planning over the entire robot’s DoFs. These challenges, known as self-collision and whole-arm collision avoidance in high-DoF robotic path planning, are both ongoing research areas. Limiting the LTS solution space in higher dimensions by considering environmental constraints is another avenue for further research. References Andreopoulos, A. & Tsotsos, J. K. (2009).
